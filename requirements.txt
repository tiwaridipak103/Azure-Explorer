Let’s frame this in real-estate market mechanics terms.

You have:

Feature: thirty_year_fixed_mortgage_rate

Frequency: Quarterly

Granularity: Market-level (likely metro / city / region)

The key question is:

How does a change in 30-year fixed mortgage rates propagate through the real estate system and eventually affect vacancy?

This depends heavily on property type (apartment, office, industrial, retail), because mortgage rates influence each sector differently.

1. What is thirty_year_fixed_mortgage_rate?
This is the average interest rate borrowers pay for a standard 30-year mortgage.

It represents the cost of long-term housing debt.

When it rises:

Monthly mortgage payments increase

Borrowing affordability declines

Housing demand often weakens

Financing costs for property acquisition rise

When it falls:

Borrowing becomes cheaper

Home purchases become more attractive

Property demand increases

It is essentially a capital cost signal for real estate.

2. Why would it affect vacancy?
Vacancy = unused real estate supply.

Mortgage rates affect vacancy through demand migration, construction incentives, and investment decisions.

The relationship is usually indirect and lagged.

Channel 1: Affordability Channel (Most Important for Apartments)
This is usually the strongest mechanism.

When mortgage rates rise
Higher rates make buying homes expensive.

Example:

Suppose a buyer wants a ₹80 lakh home.

At 6%
Monthly payment ≈ ₹48,000

At 8%
Monthly payment ≈ ₹58,700

That’s a big jump.

Many households can no longer buy.

So they:

→ Stay renters longer
→ Delay homeownership
→ Increase apartment demand

Higher apartment demand reduces vacant rental units.

So:

Mortgage rate ↑ → Rental demand ↑ → Apartment vacancy ↓

This is often observed in multifamily markets.

Real-world interpretation
If your model shows:

negative coefficient

for apartments:

that is economically intuitive.

Meaning:

Higher mortgage rates trap households in rental markets, tightening occupancy.

Channel 2: Housing Supply Freeze
When mortgage rates rise:

Developers face:

Higher construction financing costs

Lower buyer demand

Lower project feasibility

They delay new housing projects.

Less future supply enters.

Lower supply can reduce vacancy.

So:

Mortgage rate ↑ → Construction slows → Future vacancy ↓

Important:

This effect appears with long lag.

Usually:

4 to 12 quarters later

For quarterly Moody’s data, lagged features matter.

Example:

mortgage_rate_lag_4
mortgage_rate_lag_8
Channel 3: Lock-In Effect
This became very important post-2022.

When homeowners have low-rate mortgages (say 3%) and market rates jump to 7%:

They avoid selling.

Housing inventory shrinks.

Potential buyers remain renters.

Rental demand rises.

Vacancy falls.

This is another reason:

Higher mortgage rates can reduce apartment vacancy

Channel 4: Wealth / Economic Slowdown Channel
High mortgage rates often reflect tighter monetary policy.

Tighter monetary policy can cause:

Lower business expansion

Reduced hiring

Slower household formation

Lower migration

This reduces demand for space.

Then vacancy rises.

So here:

Mortgage rate ↑ → Economic slowdown → Vacancy ↑

This is the opposite direction.

This often dominates for:

Office

Retail

Industrial (sometimes)

Economic logic:

After repeated shocks, households and investors adapt.

Response elasticity weakens.

9. Mortgage Smoothness Ratio
Distinguishes orderly repricing from disorderly repricing.

rate = df['thirty_year_fixed_mortgage_rate']

df['mortgage_smoothness'] = (
    rate.rolling(8).mean() /
    (rate.diff().abs().rolling(8).sum() + 1e-6)
)
Why realistic:

Smooth repricing is easier for leasing markets to absorb.

10. Mortgage Compression Window
Measures how tightly rates are clustered recently.

df['mortgage_compression_window'] = (
    df.groupby('market')['thirty_year_fixed_mortgage_rate']
      .transform(lambda x:
          x.rolling(6).max() - x.rolling(6).min()
      )
)
Low compression:

Stable expectations

High compression:

Stress repricing phase

11. Mortgage Decision Friction
Captures indecision zone.

When rates hover in a narrow high band, participants hesitate.

df['mortgage_decision_friction'] = (
    (
        df['thirty_year_fixed_mortgage_rate'].between(6,7)
    ).astype(int)
)
This is surprisingly practical.

Many markets freeze when rates sit in uncertain middle zones.

12. Mortgage Repricing Speed Burst
Detects unusually fast adjustments.

df['mortgage_speed_burst'] = (
    df.groupby('market')['thirty_year_fixed_mortgage_rate']
      .diff(2)
      .abs()
)
Useful because:

Fast repricing causes occupancy expectation resets.

13. Mortgage Stability Confidence
Inverse uncertainty proxy.

df['mortgage_stability_confidence'] = (
    1 /
    (
        df.groupby('market')['thirty_year_fixed_mortgage_rate']
          .transform(lambda x: x.rolling(4).std())
        + 1e-6
    )
)
Higher confidence:

More leasing activity.

Lower confidence:

Delayed transactions.

14. Mortgage Turning-Point Probability
Detects likely reversal zones.

rolling_mean = (
    df.groupby('market')['thirty_year_fixed_mortgage_rate']
      .transform(lambda x: x.rolling(10).mean())
)

df['mortgage_turning_point'] = (
    abs(df['thirty_year_fixed_mortgage_rate'] - rolling_mean)
)
Large deviations often precede normalization.

Markets anticipate this.

15. Mortgage Entropy
Captures randomness of mortgage path.

Advanced but realistic.

def entropy(arr):
    hist, _ = np.histogram(arr, bins=5)
    p = hist / hist.sum()
    p = p[p > 0]
    return -(p * np.log(p)).sum()

df['mortgage_entropy'] = (
    df.groupby('market')['thirty_year_fixed_mortgage_rate']
      .transform(lambda x: x.rolling(10).apply(entropy))
)
Why useful:

Higher entropy = harder forecasting environment.

Vacancy often becomes less responsive.
