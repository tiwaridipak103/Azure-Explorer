Great feature to analyze. sp500_dividend_yield is one of those macro-financial variables that looks unrelated to real estate vacancy at first glance, but it often acts as a strong capital market regime indicator, which indirectly affects vacancy through investment behavior, financing conditions, and economic sentiment.

Let’s break down how it works in a real estate vacancy forecasting context.

1. What is sp500_dividend_yield?
It is:

\text{Dividend Yield} = \frac{\text{Annual Dividends per Share}}{\text{S&P 500 Price}}
It measures how much dividend income investors receive relative to stock prices.

High dividend yield usually means:
Stock prices have fallen

Investors are risk-averse

Market stress / uncertainty

Potential economic slowdown expectations

Low dividend yield usually means:
Stock prices are expensive

Risk appetite is high

Growth expectations strong

Capital flows toward equities

So this feature is less about dividends and more about market regime / investor sentiment / risk pricing.

2. Why would it affect real estate vacancy?
Vacancy is fundamentally demand vs supply.

sp500_dividend_yield influences vacancy through three transmission channels.

Channel A: Economic Growth Expectations
When dividend yield rises sharply:

This often means equity markets are declining.

Markets usually decline because investors expect:

weaker GDP growth

lower business expansion

weaker hiring

reduced consumer spending

These directly impact property demand.

Office
Lower hiring → less office absorption → higher vacancy

Retail
Lower spending → store closures → higher vacancy

Industrial
Lower consumption/logistics demand → weaker warehouse demand

Apartments
Job weakness → household formation slows → vacancy rises

Example:

If S&P dividend yield jumps from 1.3% to 2.2%, it often reflects broad equity repricing.

6–12 months later you may see:

slower leasing

reduced tenant expansion

vacancy increase

This lag is critical.

Channel B: Relative Yield Competition
This is very important in institutional real estate.

Investors compare:

Equity dividend yield vs Real estate cap rate

Conceptually:

\text{Spread} = \text{Cap Rate} - \text{S&P Dividend Yield}
If S&P dividend yield rises:

Stocks become more income-attractive.

Capital may rotate away from commercial real estate.

That leads to:

reduced CRE investment

delayed development/refinancing

valuation pressure

How does this affect vacancy?

Indirectly through:

Reduced capital availability
Owners may postpone repositioning / leasing improvements.

Properties become less competitive.

Vacancy can rise.

Channel C: Risk-Off Environment
High dividend yield often accompanies:

tighter credit spreads

reduced lending appetite

lower business confidence

Businesses then delay:

expansion

relocation

new leases

This reduces net absorption.

Vacancy rises.

3. Why the relationship differs by property type
This is crucial for your global model.

Apartments
Usually weaker relationship.

Because housing demand is more necessity-driven.

Unless yield spike reflects severe recession.

Office
Usually strongest relationship.

Office demand depends heavily on:

hiring

corporate confidence

expansion plans

Dividend yield often captures this.

Retail
Strong relationship.

Consumer confidence and spending closely tied to equity wealth effects.

Industrial
Moderate relationship.

Depends on whether yield spike reflects:

consumer slowdown
or

broader capital market repricing

4. Time lag matters a lot
This feature rarely impacts vacancy contemporaneously.

Likely useful lags:

1 quarter lag

2 quarter lag

4 quarter lag

Because:

Financial markets move first.

Real estate leasing reacts later.

This is classic macro lead-lag behavior.

You should test:

sp500_dividend_yield_lag1
sp500_dividend_yield_lag2
sp500_dividend_yield_lag4
Sometimes lag-2 is strongest.

5. Direction of relationship
Usually:

Higher dividend yield → Higher future vacancy
because it signals economic stress.

But not always.

Sometimes high dividend yield can coincide with:

lower rates + flight to yield assets

which can support real estate.

That’s why interaction terms matter.
